Two Hand Tide Clock — Gullane Leith-based

Why the second hand?

The main reason for the second (Sun) hand is that it shows spring and neap tides. When the Sun and Moon hands line up, at new and full moon, their pulls add together and you get big spring tides: higher highs and lower lows. When the hands point in opposite directions, at the quarter moons, they partly cancel and you get small neap tides. (In the sky the Sun and Moon are then at right angles, 90° apart; the clock hands turn twice per day, so that 90° shows as 180° on the dial.) So at a glance the clock shows whether today's tide is a big one or a small one, which a one-hand clock can't do.

Better accuracy is a secondary, marginal benefit. Adding the Sun hand cuts the average high-water timing error only from about 27 to 23 minutes, and the average height error from about 0.39 to 0.22 m (Leith, 18.6-year simulation, details below).

Air pressure

Live pressure comes from Open-Meteo every 30 minutes. 1 cm of tide per hPa from 1013 hPa.

How accurate is each clock?

Most tide clocks rely on one hand, following the Moon's gravitational pull, but this leads to errors of up to 2 h 11 min and 1.5 m (34 min and 0.42 m on average). This clock takes account of the impact of the Sun's gravitational pull, and reduces the error to up to 2 h 1 min and 0.9 m (24 min and 0.24 m on average). The online version of the clock takes account of N2 lunar distance, K1/O1 diurnal inequality, M4 shallow-water distortion and live air pressure, and this reduces the error to up to 31 min and 0.17 m (4 min and 3.5 cm on average).

The largest time errors come at neap low waters, when the tide is nearly flat. 95% of high and low waters fall within 84 min (one hand), 59 min (two hands) and 11 min (online).

Check against the official tables

Compared with the NTSLF Leith predictions for 22 Oct – 18 Nov 2026 (108 high and low waters), the online clock averages 7.9 min and 5 cm of difference (worst 33 min, 0.19 m). The mean timing bias is −1.5 min, so there is no time-zone or phase error.

Weather (not included above)

Air pressure moves the sea about 1 cm per hPa, roughly ±0.3 m from 980 to 1040 hPa, and the live pressure feed corrects for this. Wind surge isn't modelled. Whatever difference is left on the day is weather.

How it works

2.4 mNeap range, 2×(1.80−0.61)

Two centred hands: the Moon hand is M2, with Leith's real amplitude of 1.80 m, turning once every 12 h 25 m 14 s; the Sun hand is S2, amplitude 0.61 m, turning once every 12 h. The Sun hand slowly gains, so the hands line up every 14.77 days (springs) and point opposite ways in between (neaps).

Each hand's height is its amplitude times the cosine of its angle from straight up. Add the two to the 3.29 m mean level and you read the tide; the height sum table does the adding, plus the smaller terms.

The physical prototype

Moon hand (M2): a commercial "time and tide" quartz movement, one turn per 12 h 25 m.

Sun hand (S2): an ordinary 12 h hand.

Assembly: both hands centred on the same pivot, lengths in the ratio of their Leith amplitudes, 1.80 m to 0.61 m (about 3:1).
